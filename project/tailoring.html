Chapter I

The Anatomy of Tailoring

How a garment is built determines how it wears, drapes, and ages over time.

Construction Matters

True tailoring begins beneath the cloth. A canvassed jacket uses a layer of horsehair or wool canvas between the outer fabric and lining, allowing the chest to mould to the body over years of wear.

Fused (glued) construction is cheaper and common in mass-market suits. It loses shape faster and cannot be altered as gracefully. For quiet luxury, canvassed or half-canvassed is the standard.

Key Fit Points

Every element must work in harmony. One fault throws off the entire silhouette.

Shoulder

The shoulder seam should end exactly where your shoulder ends — not beyond, not short. A rope shoulder adds authority; a natural shoulder feels softer and more contemporary.

Chest & Waist

The jacket should close smoothly with a slight waist suppression. If the lapels gape when buttoned, the chest is too tight or the posture is off.

Sleeve Length

A quarter to half inch of shirt cuff should show. This small detail signals attention to proportion and is a hallmark of refined dressing.

Trouser Break

No break creates a clean modern line. A slight break is traditional and forgiving. Too much pooling at the ankle looks careless, not casual.

The Tailor's Rule

Alter what you own before you buy new. A good tailor is worth more than a new suit.

  • Take in the waist before shortening sleeves — proportion flows from the core
  • Never rush bespoke; allow three to four fittings for the canvas to settle
  • Press, do not iron, wool jackets — steam preserves the nap
  • Rest each suit at least 48 hours between wears to recover its shape
